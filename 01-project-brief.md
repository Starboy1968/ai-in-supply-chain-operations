# Project brief

## Who wrote this, and from what angle

Rashon Alsberry is a risk and strategy analyst (CompTIA Security+) moving into supply chain and third-party risk. Their background is Army IT service desk work and years running restaurant kitchens, where ordering from vendors, counting stock, and deciding what to do when a delivery didn't show up were part of every week. They are **learning supply chain operations**, not claiming to have run them. This guide is written from that position: a strategist who already understands the risk side, working out how AI fits into day-to-day supply chain decisions and where it goes wrong.

## Objective

Explain, in plain words, how a supply chain manager can use AI in six common jobs, what to check before trusting it, and how one real-world-style decision looks when you add incentives, human behavior, and AI to the picture.

## Questions this project answers

1. For each common use case, what does AI actually do, what data does it need, and what does a manager do step by step?
2. What should a manager check before acting on an AI output?
3. Where does AI fall short, and which risks does it create? (Linked to the companion [AI Supply Chain Risk Assessment](https://github.com/Starboy1968/ai-supply-chain-risk-assessment).)
4. When AI enters a decision, how do the incentives between buyers, suppliers and carriers change, and which human biases get stronger or weaker?

## Audience

- Supply chain, procurement and logistics managers who are curious about AI but don't want a data science course.
- Hiring managers looking for someone who can connect operations, risk and strategy.

## Scope

**In:** demand forecasting, supplier selection and monitoring, inventory decisions, routing and load planning, invoice and shipment anomalies, contract review; one strategic case study; limits; a low-cost way to start.

**Out:** building models, vendor product comparisons, formulas. Concepts like safety stock are explained in words, not equations.

## Conventions

- All companies, people and examples are **fictional and illustrative**. Before-and-after examples are described in words; no invented numbers or results.
- Tools are named by category ("a general AI assistant", "a spreadsheet", "a TMS"), not by vendor.
- Research is cited only where it was checked; see [sources.md](sources.md).
- Payoffs in the case study are **ranked** (best, second-best, ...), not calculated.
